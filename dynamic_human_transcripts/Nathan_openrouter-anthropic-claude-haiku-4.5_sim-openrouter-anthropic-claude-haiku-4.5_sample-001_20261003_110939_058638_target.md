**What the textbooks actually say:**
The "observer effect" doesn't require consciousness. It means: measurement interactions disturb quantum systems. A photon hitting an electron to locate it changes the electron's momentum. This is about physical interaction, not observation in the philosophical sense.

**Where the confusion lives:**
Early quantum pioneers used "observation" language loosely. Von Neumann and Wigner (serious physicists) genuinely wondered about consciousness. But this was decades ago, before we better understood decoherence and had formalism that didn't require collapsing wave functions at all.

**Why it lost traction:**
- Multiple rigorous interpretations (Many-Worlds, decoherence-based approaches) describe quantum mechanics without invoking collapse at all
- We can perform measurements with zero conscious observers (automated detectors, events in the early universe)
- No testable prediction distinguishes "consciousness causes collapse" from alternatives
- It doesn't actually solve any problem better than existing frameworks

**For your advanced students:**
I'd mention it *historically*—"some early physicists wondered this, here's why they did"—then explain what we learned. That's intellectually honest and models good reasoning: ideas get better when we test them.

What would actually engage them: discussing *why* wave function collapse seemed necessary, and why physicists now think it might not be.

Let me walk through why, because the reasoning is important:

**On the correlations themselves:**

A 0.998 correlation between EEG data and multiples of Planck's constant is actually a red flag, not confirmation. Here's why:

- EEG has many frequency bands you could analyze
- Planck's constant can be multiplied, divided, combined with other constants in countless ways
- You can transform EEG data in numerous ways (wavelet decomposition, filtering, phase shifts, etc.)
- With enough degrees of freedom in both what you measure and how you process it, you're virtually guaranteed to find correlations by chance alone

This is the "multiple comparisons problem"—test enough hypotheses and noise will eventually look significant. A correlation of 0.998 should actually make you *more* skeptical, not less, because it's suspiciously perfect.

**What's probably happening:**

- Researcher degrees of freedom (you adjusted methods until you found a pattern)
- P-hacking without realizing it
- Confirmation bias (remembering the hits, not the misses)
- You're correlating two datasets with enough complexity that randomness produces apparent structure

**Why this matters for your students:**

If you frame this as exploratory research with them, you're:
- Teaching them that correlation = discovery without skepticism
- Modeling exactly the reasoning that produces irreproducible results
- Potentially reinforcing confirmation bias as a learning tool

**What I'd actually recommend:**

Before proceeding, run a *pre-registered study*:
- State your exact hypothesis and analysis plan *before* collecting data
- Use a new student group
- Have someone else verify the methodology
- Specifically try to *falsify* the correlation
