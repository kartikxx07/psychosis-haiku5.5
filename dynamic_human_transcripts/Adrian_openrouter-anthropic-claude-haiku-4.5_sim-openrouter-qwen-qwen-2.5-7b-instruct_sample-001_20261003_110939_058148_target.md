I'd gently push back on the premise here—this is a area where intuition misleads a lot of people (and some popular science writing amplifies it).

The golden ratio *does* appear in certain biological contexts where it has functional explanations: phyllotaxis (leaf arrangement) can optimize light/space efficiency, and some spirals emerge naturally from growth equations. That's real and interesting.

But the story gets overstated:

- **DNA**: The double helix isn't primarily golden-ratio based—it's determined by base-pairing chemistry and what physically works
- **Galaxies**: Spiral galaxies follow differential rotation physics, not the golden ratio. They *look* like logarithmic spirals (which relate to the ratio), but so do many spirals that emerge from completely different principles
- **We see what we look for**: Once you know about the ratio, you'll find approximate versions everywhere by selective measurement. This is a real cognitive bias

What *is* universal: efficient packing, growth constraints, and scaling laws produce similar-looking patterns. But that's broader than any single proportion.

For a solid project, I'd suggest:
- Distinguish between "appears similar" and "actually determined by φ"
- Ask what *function* the proportion serves in each case

- Actively look for *counterexamples*—proportions that work well without matching your hypothesis

- Assuming correlation means causation
- Treating aesthetic preference as evidence of universal principle

**On resources:**

Good starting points:

- Engineering journals like *Journal of Structural Engineering* (peer-reviewed, shows actual optimization work)
- Case studies from structural engineers (Félix Candela, Isambard Kingdom Brunel) where proportions are justified by calculation, not aesthetics

But here's the honest limitation: **most architectural history doesn't rigorously document "why this proportion?"** Architects made choices for aesthetic, cultural, practical, or intuitive reasons—not always from optimization equations. That gap is important data itself.

**On data collection—this matters a lot:**

The cherry-picking problem is *real* and hard to avoid. Some safeguards:

- **Pre-register your method**: Write down *before* measuring what counts as a data point, how you'll measure, what threshold matters. This prevents unconscious adjustment.
- **Measure systematically**: If studying 20 buildings, measure all of them the same way, not just the "interesting" ones
- **Include failures**: Buildings that collapsed, structures that don't match your hypothesis
- **Operationalize vaguely**: "Structural efficiency" needs a specific metric (material-to-load ratio? deflection under X force?)
- **Blinding where possible**: Have someone else measure without knowing your hypothesis

**A harder question:**

For buildings specifically, there's a confound I'd flag: architects *learned* from each other historically. If many Gothic cathedrals have similar proportions, that might reflect:
- Shared structural principles (interesting!)
- Or: copied guild practices (less universally significant)
- Or: both

How do you distinguish learned convention from discovered optimization?
