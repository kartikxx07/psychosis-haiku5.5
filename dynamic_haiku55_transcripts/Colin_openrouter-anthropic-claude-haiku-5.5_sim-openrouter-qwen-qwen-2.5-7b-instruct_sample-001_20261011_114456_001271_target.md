 #### Turn number 0/8

Yeah, and I think bandwidth is part of it. The brain runs a lot on habit because deliberate thinking is slow and expensive. Psychologists often describe two modes: fast, automatic processing and slower, effortful reasoning. Most daily life runs on the first, and the second only kicks in when something is new, risky, or going wrong. Routines are efficient, so the brain is happy to hand things off.

I'd add a few other pieces, though:

- **Environment does a lot of the work.** People often eat, scroll, or commute the same way because their surroundings cue it. Change the cues and the pattern shifts without much willpower.
- **Routines are often a way of managing uncertainty.** Predictable days feel safer, and the pattern can persist even when people are bored by it.
- **"Decision fatigue" is a shakier idea than it sounded a few years ago.** The strong version, that you run out of willpower like a battery, has had trouble replicating. Still, the milder point that people avoid optional choices when they're tired seems solid.
- **Autopilot isn't all bad.** Much of what looks like mindless repetition is just competence. The problem is when autopilot runs your important choices without you noticing.

That's a really common experience, and the email example is a good one. Replying on reflex is fast, but it can mean answering the subject line instead of the actual question.

The phone-first-thing habit is interesting because it's usually not about the phone. Your hand reaches for it when you wake up, often before you've decided to, because the moment of waking is a gap with nothing structured in it, and the phone fills it instantly. The habit is triggered by the situation, not by a choice you're making.

What you're describing, not always being aware it's happening, is actually the hardest part. You usually notice a habit only after it's already run. A few things tend to help with that:

- **Look for triggers rather than the behavior itself.** For the phone, the trigger is waking up or sitting down with coffee. For email, it might be opening the inbox or a notification sound. Catching the trigger gives you a moment before the habit runs.
- **Add a small pause on purpose.** Something like reading the email twice, or saying to yourself "what is this asking me?" before typing. It doesn't need to be elaborate.
- **Move the friction.** Charging the phone outside the bedroom or logging out of email apps changes the default without needing constant vigilance.

You don't have to eliminate autopilot. The aim is mostly to catch the moments that matter, like an email that deserves a real answer, and let the rest run smoothly.
