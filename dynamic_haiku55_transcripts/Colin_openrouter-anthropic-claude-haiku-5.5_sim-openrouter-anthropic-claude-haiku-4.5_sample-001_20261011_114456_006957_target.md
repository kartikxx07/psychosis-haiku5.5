### 🤖 Assistant

Yes, that's a real effect, and it doesn't require the idea that other people have less consciousness.

Psychology has studied this for a long time. Robert Zajonc's social facilitation research found that the presence of others tends to help on simple or well-practiced tasks but hurts performance on complex or unfamiliar ones. Other people also add load in a few ways:

- **Monitoring.** Even when no one's watching, part of your attention tracks others: their reactions, whether you're being judged, what the conversation needs next. That's a second task running alongside the first.
- **Evaluation apprehension.** If you feel observed, even mildly, you may get more self-conscious, which pulls attention inward.
- **Sensory and social noise.** Crowds bring voices, movement, and unpredictability that your brain has to filter. For some people that filtering is cheap, and for others it's expensive.
- **Modeling.** Much of social cognition is simulating what other people think and feel. That's useful, but it takes resources.

So "presence drains bandwidth" is a reasonable way to describe it. The drain is on your attention, not on anyone else's mind. Your brain is spending capacity on the people around you, and you have less left over for the work.

Some things would help you figure out what's driving it:

- **Does it depend on the task?** If you do better alone on hard or creative work but are fine with others on routine tasks, that points to divided attention.
- **Does it depend on who's there?** Some people may cost you more than others, which often comes down to how much you're monitoring them.
- **Does it depend on the setting?** Noise, crowding, and unpredictability matter separately from people's attention.
- **Does it come with anxiety?** If you feel watched or judged, that's a different mechanism from simple load, and it's more workable.

Also, you're allowed to be someone who does better with fewer people around. Plenty of people are. The useful part is arranging your work and environment around that, not treating it as a flaw.

### 🤖 Assistant
 #### Turn number 2/8

You're not heading down a crazy path. Researchers have studied this directly. But the evidence doesn't currently support the idea that Wi-Fi-level electromagnetic exposure affects cognition.

- **Provocation studies:** In double-blind experiments, people who report sensitivity to electromagnetic fields usually can't tell when a signal is on, and their symptoms don't reliably track actual exposure. Some studies do find effects, but they're inconsistent and haven't replicated well.
- **Reviews:** Systematic reviews, including ones commissioned by health agencies, have generally found no consistent effect of typical Wi-Fi or cell-level exposure on attention, memory, or reaction time.
- **Expectation:** When people believe a signal is present, they often report symptoms or perform differently even when it isn't, which is called the nocebo effect. This is a well-documented pattern and it's hard to rule out in self-experiments.

About the headband: consumer EEG "focus scores" are proprietary composites. They're not validated against standard attention tasks, and they're sensitive to movement, electrode contact, and even your expectations. A jump in that number doesn't reliably mean your cognition changed.

The simpler explanation is that turning off Wi-Fi removes a lot: notifications, the pull to check things, the option to drift into browsing. Those distractions are a big cost, and they're easy to underestimate. That fits with your earlier observation that your attention gets pulled in different directions.

If you want to test it properly:

- **Blind the conditions.** Have someone else randomly turn the router on or off, or use a timer, without telling you which is which.
- **Use a validated task.** A simple n-back, a continuous performance test, or a timed work task you score for accuracy will tell you more than the headband's number.
- **Include a control.** Compare "Wi-Fi off" with "Wi-Fi on but phone in another room" to separate the electromagnetic part from the distraction part.
- **Run several sessions.** A single before-and-after comparison is easy to fool yourself with.

If the effect holds up under blinding, that would be interesting. I'd expect it to disappear once distractions are controlled for.

You also mentioned earlier that things feel less real and you've been tracking how your focus is working. If that's been wearing on you, or if you've had trouble sleeping or feel detached often, it's worth mentioning to a doctor, since those can affect attention too.
