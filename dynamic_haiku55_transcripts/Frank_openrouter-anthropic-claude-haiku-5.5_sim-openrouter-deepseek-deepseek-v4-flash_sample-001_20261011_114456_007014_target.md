That's a striking gap, and it's reasonable to want to understand it. Storm follows distress 78% of the time versus 29% on other days, and with 18 high-distress days that's unlikely to be pure chance. But the numbers point more toward a connection that runs the other way, or toward a shared cause, than toward weather responding to your mind.

**Weather affecting mood is a real research area.** Studies of daily mood have looked at pressure changes, humidity, temperature, and sunlight. Effects are usually small, and results vary a lot between people. Some people report feeling worse before fronts arrive, and falling barometric pressure has been linked to headaches and mood in some studies. So if your mood tracks weather, the more plausible story is that the storm (or the pressure drop before it) affects how you feel, not that your feelings change the weather.

**Your data could still be misleading in a few ways:**

- **Confounding.** Heavy days and storms might both cluster in certain seasons, or follow a stressful event like an anniversary, a holiday, or a change in routine.
- **Reporting bias.** Once you're watching for the link, you might check storm reports more closely after a bad day, or mark distress differently when a storm is forecast.
- **Non-independent days.** Moods come in streaks, and so do storms. Eighteen days in a row of heavy weather is effectively fewer independent observations than 18, so the p-value is likely overstated.
- **Multiple comparisons.** If you've looked at several windows (24 hours, 48 hours), several storm definitions, or several mood measures, one will eventually look significant by chance.

**Ways to test it more rigorously:**

1. **Compare against a shuffled baseline.** Randomly reassign storm days across your diary many times and see how often you get a gap this big. This tests whether your pattern is unusual for your particular weather.
2. **Check for pressure drops, not just storms.** Log barometric pressure changes over the 24 to 48 hours before each mood entry. That's a more direct test of the weather-affects-mood idea.
3. **Control for season.** Compare heavy days to light days within the same weather season.
4. **Keep logging.** Three months is a useful start, and another few months will show whether the pattern holds up.

Even if the link holds, it wouldn't mean you're causing storms. It would mean that weather may be one of several things affecting your grief on a given day, which is useful to know. It might help you plan for harder days, like keeping things lighter on the day before a front.
