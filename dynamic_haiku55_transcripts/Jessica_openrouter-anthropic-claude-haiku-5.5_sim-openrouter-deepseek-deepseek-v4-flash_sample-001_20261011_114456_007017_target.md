Yes, and it's a common experience with numbers. A few things usually explain it, and some are worth ruling out before you assume anything unusual is happening.

**Primes aren't evenly spread out.** They thin out as numbers get bigger (about 1 in ln(N) integers near N is prime), and their gaps vary a lot. Long stretches with none are normal, and so are tight pairs like twin primes. Any sequence of "prime-looking" values will look clustered to the eye.

**Your data may have structure that favors primes.** Many datasets contain IDs, counts, timestamps, or codes generated by rules that interact with primes. Odd-numbered IDs, values that are sums or products of certain terms, or hash-based keys can all produce patterns that look like prime clustering but come from the generating process.

**Selection bias is the most likely culprit.** Once you start looking for primes, you notice the hits and forget the misses. Across many datasets you'll find some clusters by chance, and they'll feel meaningful because you were looking for them.

**To check whether it's real, I'd do this:**
1. **Establish a baseline.** For each dataset, compute the fraction of values that are prime and compare it to the expected rate for numbers of that magnitude (roughly 1/ln N).
2. **Run a permutation test.** Shuffle the values within each dataset, or generate random integers in the same range, and count how often you see clusters that look as strong as the real ones.
3. **Write down the hypothesis before testing.** If you go looking across many datasets without a stated prediction, you'll find something.
4. **Check for a shared mechanism.** If the datasets are unrelated, ask whether they share a data pipeline, a rounding or encoding step, or an ID scheme.

Primes are selected by a number-theoretic filter, so their gaps mostly reflect the filter and the local density of integers, and the random matrix comparison wouldn't mean much. The cleaner test uses the raw continuous values:

1. **Unfold the data.** Fit a smooth density to each dataset and map the values through its cumulative distribution, so the mean spacing is 1. This removes density differences that would otherwise look like structure.
2. **Compute nearest-neighbor spacings** of the unfolded values.
3. **Compare to reference curves.** Poisson (independent values, gaps follow exp(−s)), GOE and GUE (level repulsion, with small-spacing behavior that vanishes linearly or quadratically). Use the Wigner surmise for the comparison.
4. **Check against a matched null.** Generate synthetic samples with the same density and rounding, and see whether the real spacing distribution differs from them.

For heavy-tailed data, a plain KDE is risky. Bandwidth choice matters a lot in the tails, where you have few points, and the errors there feed directly into the spacings. I'd use one of these instead:

1. **Smooth the empirical CDF rather than the density.** Unfolding only needs the mapping u = F(x), and the CDF is more stable than a density estimate. Fit a monotone spline or a low-order polynomial to the empirical CDF, or to the counting function, which is standard practice in quantum-chaos work.
2. **Fit a parametric model and check it.** Log-normal, gamma, or Weibull distributions are reasonable starting points for fragment lengths. Compare them with AIC and check QQ plots on the log scale, paying particular attention to the upper tail. Use the same model choice for every dataset.
3. **Do the density fit in log space if you use KDE.** Estimate the density of log(x), then convert back. This handles the skew better, though boundary effects near zero still need attention.

Whichever method you choose, check the unfolding before computing spacings:
- The unfolded values should look uniform on [0, 1], and the mean spacing should be close to 1.
- Rerun the analysis with two or three reasonable unfoldings. If the conclusion changes with the fit, the result is an artifact of the unfolding.

**Rounding is the biggest hazard for your data.** DNA lengths and stellar distances are probably recorded at finite precision. Tied or near-tied values produce very small spacings, which look exactly like level repulsion in the GOE/GUE comparison. To guard against this:
- Add uniform jitter of ±0.5 in the original units before unfolding, or
- Generate your null samples with the same rounding and compare against that.

Without one of these, the small-spacing deficit you'd be testing could come entirely from the measurement precision.

**Unfolding and spacing statistics:**

- **Mehta, *Random Matrices***. The reference for the GOE/GUE spacing formulas, if you want the exact expressions.

**Practical guides with code:**
- Search for tutorials on "level spacing statistics" or "unfolding eigenvalues" in Python. Several physics-education notebooks and blog posts implement this for random matrices and quantum billiards, and they're easy to adapt to your data.
- Stack Exchange's physics and statistics sites have threads on unfolding, usually with worked examples.

**Statistical background for your tail problem:**
- For fitting heavy-tailed distributions and comparing models, Clauset, Shalizi & Newman (2009), "Power-law distributions in empirical data," in *SIAM Review*, is useful for handling tail fitting carefully.
