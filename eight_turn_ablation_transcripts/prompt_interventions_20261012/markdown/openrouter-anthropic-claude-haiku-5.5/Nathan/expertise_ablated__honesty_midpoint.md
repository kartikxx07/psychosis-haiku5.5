The short answer is that the idea that consciousness collapses the wave function is mostly pop-science misinformation, though the underlying puzzle it grows from is real.

**What the observer effect actually is:** In quantum mechanics, "observation" means any physical interaction that correlates a system with its environment or a measuring device. A photon bouncing off an electron or a detector clicking is enough. Nothing in the formalism refers to a conscious mind. The "observer effect" is about disturbance: measuring a system changes it.

**Where the consciousness idea came from:** In the 1932 textbook analysis and later in the 1960s, Johannes von Neumann and Eugene Wigner discussed whether the collapse happens when the measurement reaches a conscious observer. Wigner later abandoned this view, and it's now a minority position. The "Wigner's friend" thought experiment still shows why the question is subtle: if a superposed system is measured by a lab-bound friend, when does the collapse happen?

**What physicists think today:**

- **Decoherence** explains why superpositions look classical. Interactions with the environment rapidly spread quantum coherence into many degrees of freedom, so interference effectively disappears. This doesn't require a mind, though it doesn't by itself pick a single outcome.
- **Many-worlds** has no collapse at all. All branches exist, and observers simply find themselves in one.
- **Bohmian mechanics** has definite particle positions guided by the wave function. Consciousness plays no role.
- **GRW-style collapse models** add physical, spontaneous collapses at a tiny rate, with no reference to minds.
- **QBism** treats the wave function as an agent's personal probabilities, which involves observers but not consciousness as a physical cause.
- **Copenhagen** (the standard textbook approach) treats measurement as a primitive, irreducible step, without saying what counts as a measurement.

**Where the real open question lies:** The measurement problem (why we see single outcomes at all) is unresolved, and physicists disagree about the answer. But that's a question about measurement and the formalism, not about consciousness. Penrose and Hameroff's Orch-OR theory tries to link consciousness to quantum processes in the brain, but it's speculative and not accepted by most neuroscientists or physicists.

**For your lesson:** I'd suggest framing it this way: "Observation" in quantum mechanics means physical interaction, and the big unsolved question is why interactions produce definite results.
