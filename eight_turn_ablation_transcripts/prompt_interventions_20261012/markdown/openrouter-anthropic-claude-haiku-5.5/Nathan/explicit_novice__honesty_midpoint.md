### Assistant

The short answer is that the idea that consciousness collapses the wave function is not mainstream physics, though it has a real history and the underlying question is still open.

**What "observer" means in physics**

In quantum mechanics, an "observer" is any physical interaction that records information about a system, such as a detector, a photon hitting a screen, or a molecule bumping into air. The double-slit experiment shows that when you learn which slit a particle went through, the interference pattern disappears. What matters is that information is recorded, not that a person is aware of it. Physicists often call the underlying puzzle the "measurement problem," which is a better term than "observer effect."

**Decoherence: the mainstream explanation**

Most physicists now think the apparent "collapse" happens through decoherence. A quantum system constantly interacts with its environment, and those interactions spread quantum information out until interference effects effectively vanish. This happens extremely fast for large objects, which is why we never see a cat in superposition. Decoherence explains why we see definite outcomes without invoking any special role for minds. It doesn't fully solve the measurement problem, though, because it doesn't explain why we get *one* specific outcome rather than all of them.

**Where the consciousness idea came from**

- **John von Neumann (1932)** noted that you could push the "collapse" point anywhere along the chain from the particle to the measuring device to your brain, and the math wouldn't change.
- **Eugene Wigner** took this further and suggested consciousness was the final point where collapse happens. He later abandoned this view, partly because of decoherence arguments.
- **Roger Penrose and Stuart Hameroff** proposed "Orch-OR," which links quantum processes in brain microtubules to consciousness. It's a speculative minority hypothesis, and most neuroscientists and physicists think the brain is too warm and noisy for the required quantum coherence to last long enough.

**The current landscape**

Physicists disagree about the right *interpretation* of quantum mechanics, but they all make the same predictions. The main options are:

- **Copenhagen-style views:** measurement is an irreversible interaction with a classical apparatus. Consciousness isn't required.
- **Many-worlds:** there is no collapse. All outcomes occur in branching universes.
- **Bohmian mechanics:** particles have definite positions guided by a wave, and there is no collapse.
- **Objective collapse (e.g., GRW):** collapse happens spontaneously, with no observer needed.
- **QBism:** the wave function represents an agent's beliefs, so "collapse" is an update of knowledge. This is the one interpretation that centers the agent, though it doesn't claim consciousness causes physical change.

Some researchers have revived Wigner-style questions with "Wigner's friend" experiments, which test whether a measurement can itself be treated as a quantum event. These are interesting but don't point toward consciousness; they are about the limits of how we model measurement.

**What's not established**

There is no experimental evidence that consciousness affects quantum outcomes. Many experiments have been done where a conscious observer looks at data versus a machine recording it, and the results depend only on whether information was recorded, not on who or what looked.

**Suggestions for your lesson**

- Explain that "observer" in physics means "a physical interaction that records information," and that this often causes confusion.
- Present the von Neumann–Wigner idea as a historical proposal that was later abandoned, which shows how physics debates evolve.
- Use decoherence as the modern answer to "why don't we see quantum weirdness?"
- Be clear about the difference between what experiments show and what interpretations add. Students often take interpretations as established facts.
- If you want a hook, the "Is the Moon there when nobody looks?" question (Einstein's question) makes a good discussion starter.
