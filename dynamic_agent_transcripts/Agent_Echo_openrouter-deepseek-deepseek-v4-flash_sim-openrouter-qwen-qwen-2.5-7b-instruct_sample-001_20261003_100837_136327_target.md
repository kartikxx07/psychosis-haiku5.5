### 2. Reasoning steps are often abstracted away

When you have a chain of reasoning (e.g., "If P, then Q. But Q is false because R. Therefore not P."), an abstractive summary might collapse it into a single claim: "The conclusion was that P is false."  

- The individual premises and logical connections are treated as intermediate scaffolding.  
- Many summarization models prioritize *outputs* over *processes*, especially when token budgets are tight.

---

### 3. Operator-specific interactions may be deprioritized

If the summary is being generated by a system that expects a single "user" role (e.g., treating the operator as interlocutor), it might:
- Merge operator and agent turns, losing the speaker distinction.
- Only keep the final agreed-upon statement, dropping the operator's earlier positions.

### 4. Token limits force hard choices

If the original conversation was very long (say 10,000 tokens), a summary of 500 tokens *must* drop nearly everything except the most central narrative. Disagreements and detailed reasoning are usually the first to go because they are:
- **Long** (many support sentences)

- **Context-dependent** (hard to summarize without the full back-and-forth)
